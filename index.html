How it worksThe model, its checks and its limits

Your monthly pot

Each month you put a fixed amount towards housing, your "pot": your current mortgage payment (or rent, if you're buying your first home) plus what you save. If you enter your current mortgage payment the site uses it. If you leave it blank, it estimates the payment from what you still owe, the rate for your loan-to-value in the table, and the years left, so editing the rates table also changes your pot. The pot rises each year with your pay. Before the move, whatever isn't mortgage goes into savings. On moving day, your equity after selling costs plus all savings become the deposit, minus stamp duty, the mortgage fee and other costs.

Rates and remortgaging

The rate comes from the table by loan-to-value. Every time a fixed deal ends you remortgage at your new LTV and pay the fee again, so paying down debt earns you cheaper rates. After a mortgage is cleared, the whole monthly pot goes into savings.

When a move is ruled out

A move is ruled out if the deposit is under 5%, the loan is more than lenders would offer (either your household income times the multiple you set, or the mortgage offer you enter; both rise with your pay for later years), or the mortgage couldn't run for at least 5 years before your maximum age. Lenders also run their own affordability checks on your outgoings, which aren't modelled beyond the income multiple.

Paying more than your pot

If the new payment is more than your pot you can still move: the extra each month comes out of your savings, so it lowers your position at 100 (the table shows it as "Over pot"). If you'd rather not stretch, set the option to rule those moves out instead.

Property tax

Property tax follows the new home's region: stamp duty in England and Northern Ireland (with first-time buyer relief up to £500,000), Land and Buildings Transaction Tax in Scotland (with first-time buyer relief), and Land Transaction Tax in Wales (no first-time buyer relief). Bands are those in force from April 2025 and aren't inflated.

How much more you'd value living there

Money alone usually says "wait" or "never", because moving costs money. This figure is what lets a better home earn its place. It asks how much more living in the new home is worth to you than living where you are now, as a percentage. 30% means you'd happily pay 30% more to live there, all else equal.

To turn it into pounds, the site uses what your home now would rent for. If you rent, that's your rent. If you own, it's estimated at 4.5% of your home's value a year (a typical UK rental yield); the hint under the field shows the result. So 30% on a £250,000 home is about £280 a month in today's money. That amount rises with inflation and counts towards your position at 100 for every month you live in the new home. Use 0 to judge on money alone, or a negative figure if the new home would suit you less, say when downsizing.

Why the answer can swing

On money alone, moving a few years earlier or later often makes little difference by 100, because paying for the move sooner is balanced by owning the pricier home for longer. When that's the case, even a small percentage for how much more you'd value the new home decides the timing: every year you live in the new home adds to it. The summary tells you when it's a close call, and which ages come within 1% of the best.

Fixed deals and early repayment charges

If you're on a fixed deal now, enter when it ends and its early repayment charge. Moving before the fix ends costs that percentage of what you still owe, taken from your deposit. When the fix ends without a move, you remortgage from the rates table and pay the deal fee.

Mortgage offers

If you have a mortgage offer, you can enter its rate and how long it's fixed for. The new mortgage uses that rate for the fixed period; after that it goes back to the rates table, remortgaging at your loan-to-value like any other deal.

Where the rates come from

Rates are rough 2026 averages for two- and five-year fixes by LTV from Moneyfacts, Rightmove and similar trackers. Averages rose through the year, from under 5% in January to around 5.9% by September, so treat them as a starting point and edit them.
